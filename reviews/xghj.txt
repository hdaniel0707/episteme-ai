Paper Summary:
This paper introduces LogJudge, which detects AI-written text based on an LLM judge’s probabilities over human/machine answer tokens. FusionJudge then averages this score with a separately elicited numerical probability. The paper introduces the Manugram benchmark. Its finding is that answer-token probabilities break ties in verbalized scores and improve recall at low false-positive rates.

Presentation: 2: Generally understandable, but requires substantial effort in places.
Informativeness: 1: I learned little or nothing new from this paper.
Soundness: 1: I have significant concerns about the paper’s soundness that do not appear easy to address.
Critical Strengths:
Focusing on low false-positive rates is a practically relevant consideration for the given cost of incorrectly flagging human writing.
The tie-breaking ablation is helpful to get evidences regarding the mechanism behind the gains observed.
The benchmark and independent analysis of AI-polished human writing may have some potential.
Critical Weaknesses:
The core interpretation is not well grounded. The probability of generating “human” in a given prompt is not necessarily a calibrated probability of human authorship. It can be used as a classification score, but the paper does not demonstrate its interpretation as an authorship probability. Characterizing the method as recovering a finer underlying judgment thus goes beyond what is supported by the evidence.
The comparison doesn't isolate the readout mechanism enough. BaseJudge and LogJudge use different output instructions, and different calls. Their difference may have been captured by instruction-induced differences in judgment, numerical quantization or inference variability, instead of different renderings of the same judgment. In particular, BaseJudge requests 2 decimal places explicitly. Higher-precision output, repeated elicitation, and different label verbalizations will be needed to understand the degree to which the improvement is anchored to this baseline setup.
The fusion is not well-motivated. Two scores in ([0,1]) don't define a common probability scale, or motivate equal weighting. The tie-breaking analysis describes some of the gain, but doesn't justify the probabilistic interpretation of averaging. FusionJudge is better justified as a heuristic score ensemble, than as a principled probability estimate.
The evaluation does not demonstrate deployment-level false-positive control or domain robustness. Main results use thresholds chosen retrospectively on the evaluated human texts, and backbone-selection uses the evaluation splits as well. It is unclear how performance with independently-calibrated, fixed thresholds would be. Moreover, the domain-shift experiment alters the supervised detector’s training data while the evaluation slices remain fixed. It turns out that the zero-shot curves are constant by construction, which certainly does not show robustness to altering their test distribution.
Methodological novelty and reliability evidence is still scarce. Answer-token probability readout has recognized precedents, and the fusion is a straightforward average. More robust task-specific contribution beyond enhanced score resolution is desired. Appendix V also notes large score shifts for a minority of repeated calls, but assesses decision agreement at .5 instead of actual low-FPR operating thresholds, leaving deployment stability undecided.
Questions For Authors:
Do the gains hold when BaseJudge outputs higher-precision probabilities or when repeated estimates are averaged?
What are the FPR and TPR achieved using independently calibrated thresholds that are transferred between domains?
How sensitive are those decisions that are made at or close to the operating thresholds to label wording, label order, and repeated API-calls?
