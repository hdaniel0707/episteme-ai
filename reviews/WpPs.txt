Paper Summary:
This paper proposes LogJudge, a zero-shot AI-text detector that derives detection scores from label-token probabilities at the answer position, and FusionJudge, which combines these scores with the model's explicitly stated probabilities. The authors also introduce the Manugram benchmark and report that both methods outperform the evaluated baselines on average in oracle TPR@1%FPR.

Critical Strengths:
Simple and practical approach. The methods require no additional training and can use the top-k token probabilities available through commercial APIs.
Focus on low false-positive rates. The evaluation emphasizes a practically relevant operating regime, and the analysis of score ties near the decision threshold offers useful insight into the performance gains.
Valuable benchmark contribution. Manugram covers multiple domains, generators, and paraphrased texts, providing a useful resource for evaluating AI-text detectors.
Critical Weaknesses:
The treatment of residual probability mass requires targeted validation. Assigning a neutral value of 0.5 to probability mass outside the top-k window or unmapped to valid labels needs further justification. Please report label-absence rates and residual-mass distributions near the decision threshold, and evaluate how different k values and residual-mass treatments affect TPR@1%FPR.
The gains have not been sufficiently disentangled from output precision and prompt format. BaseJudge is instructed to output only two decimal places, whereas LogJudge uses finer-grained token probabilities and different output instructions, so the gains cannot yet be attributed entirely to "different readouts of the same judgment." Please increase BaseJudge's output precision, repeat the comparison under the same oracle TPR@1%FPR protocol, and report the number of distinct scores and the sizes of tied groups near the threshold.
The evaluation does not sufficiently support claims of cross-domain stability or low-FPR deployment. Oracle TPR@1%FPR is a reasonable measure of discriminative ability, but its threshold depends on human-class labels from the evaluation set, and backbone selection also uses the evaluation data. The domain-shift experiment fixes the test set and varies only the supervised methods' training sources, which does not establish that zero-shot methods remain stable when the test distribution changes.
